How to grade short-answer questions without punishing typos

23 September 2026 · 7 min read

Written questions are the ones worth asking. They are also the ones most teachers quietly stop asking, because marking thirty of them by hand costs an evening and auto-marking them costs your credibility the first time a student who clearly knew the answer gets a zero for a missing letter.

That trade-off is not actually forced. It exists because most quiz software treats a written answer as a string to compare, and a string comparison has exactly two outcomes. Real answers do not.

Why exact matching fails on written answers

Consider one question: "Who is credited with the theorem relating the sides of a right-angled triangle?" Here are four answers a class will actually give:

  • Pythagoras — the key.
  • Pythagorus — knows it, misspelled it.
  • pythagoras — knows it, typed on a phone with autocapitalise off.
  • Pythagoras of Samos — knows it better than the key does.

Exact matching scores the first one and fails the other three. The student who wrote more than you asked for is punished for it. That is not strictness — it is measuring typing rather than knowledge, and it produces a mark you cannot defend to a parent.

The second-order cost is worse. Once you know the auto-marking is unreliable, you have to read every answer to find its mistakes, including the ones it got right. You now do more work than if you had marked the whole thing by hand.

The principle: decide what "correct" means per question

There is no single right strictness. On a spelling test, recieve is wrong and must be wrong. On a history quiz, a misspelled name is a spelling slip in a question that was not about spelling. The same tolerance cannot serve both, so the choice has to sit on the question, not on the app.

In practice that gives five useful settings.

1. Exact match, normalised

Compare letter for letter, but first fold away case and punctuation. New York, USA and new york usa become the same answer, which they obviously are. This is the sensible default for single-token factual answers.

2. Case-sensitive exact

Keep capitals and punctuation and require both. This is the spelling-test rule, and the rule for anything where HCl and hcl genuinely differ — chemical formulae, code identifiers, case-marked notation.

Use it deliberately and rarely. It is the setting most likely to produce an unfair zero, which is fine when the capital letter is the assessment and indefensible when it isn't.

3. Typo tolerance with a threshold you control

Score how close the answer is to the key rather than whether it equals it, and accept anything above a line you set. Pythagorus against Pythagoras is one character out of ten — obviously the same answer. Plato is not.

The threshold matters. Set it too tight and you are back to exact matching; too loose and genuinely different words start matching each other. A useful instinct: loosen it for long proper nouns, tighten it for short words, where a single character is a much larger share of the whole.

4. Keyword coverage, for explanations

Some questions want reasoning, and reasoning cannot be compared to a key at all. What you can do is list the ideas that have to be present and set how many are enough.

Ask "Explain what plants need for photosynthesis" and list chlorophyll, sunlight, carbon dioxide, water. An answer that hits three of four is clearly not a blank page, and treating it as one is absurd. Set the bar at "most" and it passes; set it at "all" when the omission genuinely matters.

This is the setting that makes "explain why" questions viable at scale, and it is the one most teachers do not realise exists.

5. Numeric equivalence

Numbers should be compared as numbers. 7, seven and 7.0 are one answer, and so are 1500 and one thousand five hundred. Any tool comparing those as text will mark two of the three wrong, which is purely an artefact of the implementation and has nothing to do with the student.

The rule that matters more than all five

Never let the algorithm mark a written answer wrong on its own. If a rule cannot settle an answer confidently, that answer should go into a review queue for a human — not into the zero column.

This single constraint changes the economics completely. An auto-grader that can only be right or wrong has to be checked everywhere. An auto-grader that can also say "I'm not sure, you look" only has to be checked where it asked for help.

In a thirty-student class the unsure pile is usually four or five answers. Those are exactly the interesting ones — the unusual phrasings, the partly-right reasoning, the answer that is better than your key. They are the answers that deserved a teacher in the first place.

Practical advice for writing the questions

  • List every acceptable answer, not just your favourite. If H2O and water are both fine, say so up front rather than discovering it on script eleven.
  • Ask for one thing. "Name the process and explain why it matters" is two questions wearing a coat, and no single rule can grade it.
  • Say how much you want. "In one word" removes an entire category of near-miss.
  • Reuse your review decisions. When you accept an answer you did not anticipate, add it to the accepted list for next year.
  • Prefer several loose rules to one tight one. A question can carry typo tolerance and numeric equivalence and accept whichever fires.

What you get back

Done properly, the marking pile for a twenty-question class test becomes a handful of answers rather than six hundred, and the marks you hand back are ones you can explain. More usefully, written questions stop being the expensive option — which means you can go back to asking them.
