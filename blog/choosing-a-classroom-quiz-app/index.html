How to choose a classroom quiz app

23 September 2026 · 9 min read

Quiz tools all look similar on a comparison page. They have question types, they have a join code, they have reports. Then you use one for a term and discover that the thing which actually mattered was never on the list.

These are the eight questions worth answering before you commit, roughly in order of how much regret they prevent. None of them are about the number of question types.

1. What happens to a written answer that is nearly right?

This is the question that separates tools more than any other, and it is almost never answered on a feature page — "short answer questions" tells you the format exists, not how it is judged.

There are three possible behaviours, and they are very different:

  • Marked wrong. A student who typed one letter incorrectly scores the same as one who left it blank.
  • Marked right on a fuzzy guess. Better, but now you cannot trust the right-column either.
  • Handed to you. The tool says "I could not settle this" and puts it in a review queue.

Only the third is actually useful, because only the third lets you trust the answers it did mark. Test it with a deliberate typo before you decide anything else.

2. What happens when the network drops mid-quiz?

School Wi-Fi fails when thirty devices join at once — this is normal, not exceptional. The question is whether the tool treats a ten-second gap as a fatal error or as nothing.

Test it in ninety seconds: start a quiz on your own phone, turn on airplane mode, answer two more questions, turn it off again. Either the answers were saved and sync, or you have just seen what your class will experience.

Ask specifically: are answers saved on the device before they are sent to the server? "Works offline" on a marketing page often means the app opens offline, not that a quiz survives.

3. What is actually free, and for how long?

"Free" covers a wide range. Worth establishing precisely:

  • Is there a cap on participants per quiz?
  • Are question types restricted on the free tier — is short answer a paid feature?
  • Are reports and exports free, or the main paid upsell?
  • Is it free permanently, or free while they grow?

The common pattern is that everything works beautifully in a demo and the export button asks for money at the end of your first real test. Check the export before you need it.

4. Do students see advertising?

If the tool is free and has no paid tier funding it, something is paying for it. Sometimes that is ads shown to children during a lesson, which is a decision you are making on their behalf whether or not you meant to.

Look at the app as a student sees it, not as a teacher. The two views are frequently not the same.

5. How much administration does joining require?

Some tools want a class roster, imported in advance, maintained through the year. Others want a code on the board. The difference is roughly an afternoon per term, and the roster approach only pays off if you need per-student history across a whole year.

Be honest about which you need. Most teachers who set up rosters do it because the tool asked, not because they wanted it.

6. Can you get your results out?

Ask specifically: PDF, spreadsheet, or nothing? And does the export include the answers as students typed them, or only the scores?

The moment this matters is a parent meeting, a moderation exercise, or an appeal about a grade. "The scores are in the app" is a bad answer in all three. So is an export that shows 6/10 without showing which six.

7. What happens to the student data?

Three concrete things to check, all of which should be answerable from the privacy policy in under five minutes:

  • What is stored, and where?
  • Is it sold, shared, or used for advertising profiles?
  • Can an account and its data be deleted, and by whom?

If the privacy policy is vague enough that you cannot answer those, that is itself the answer. Your school may also have a formal requirement here — worth checking before you introduce a tool rather than after.

8. Does speed change the score?

Many quiz tools weight marks by how quickly someone answered. For a review game that is the point. For assessment it is a measurement error: you are rewarding fast confident guessing over careful thinking, and systematically disadvantaging students who process more slowly or read less quickly — which is rarely what you intended to measure.

Check whether it can be turned off. Sometimes it cannot.

A ten-minute evaluation

Rather than reading comparison pages, do this with any candidate tool:

  1. Build a four-question quiz: one multiple choice, one true/false, one short written answer, one numeric.
  2. Take it yourself on a phone. Answer the written one with a deliberate typo, and the numeric one in words ("seven" not "7").
  3. Turn on airplane mode for one question.
  4. Submit and look at what you are shown. Was the typo marked wrong, right, or flagged? Was "seven" accepted?
  5. Try to export the result.

That takes about ten minutes and tells you more than an hour of feature comparison, because it exercises exactly the four things that go wrong in practice.

Matching the tool to the job

There is no single best quiz app, because "quiz" covers two quite different activities:

Engagement

A fast, loud review game. Leaderboards and speed scoring are features here, not bugs. Written answers barely matter. Choose for atmosphere.

Assessment

A test whose result you will act on. Grading quality, offline resilience, exports and fairness dominate. Choose for trustworthiness.

Plenty of teachers use one tool for each, and that is a perfectly reasonable answer. The mistake is using an engagement tool for assessment and then wondering why the marks need checking.
