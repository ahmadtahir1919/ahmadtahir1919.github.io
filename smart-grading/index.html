THE GRADING ENGINE

A missing letter is not a wrong answer.

Most quiz tools compare a written answer to a string and mark anything else zero. Quizoma gives you five ways to decide what "correct" means for each question — and hands you anything it is not sure about instead of guessing.

The problem this solves

A student who knows the answer types Pythagoras as Pythagorus. A trainee writes seven where the answer key says 7. Someone gives a perfectly good three-sentence explanation that happens not to contain the exact phrase you wrote down at 11pm the night before.

In a string-comparison quiz tool, all three score zero. Then you spend the evening un-doing the marking, which is worse than not having auto-marking at all — you now have to check every answer and distrust the ones it got right.

The five matching rules

Each written or fill-in-the-blank question carries its own rule. You can also give a question several rules at once, and the answer is accepted if any of them is satisfied.

1. Exact match

Letter for letter, after case and punctuation are normalised away. Use it when the answer is a single unambiguous token and you want no interpretation at all.

"New York, USA"→"new york usa" ·accepted

2. Case-sensitive exact

The strict one: capitalisation and punctuation are both preserved and both must match. This is the rule for a spelling test, a code identifier, or anything where don't and dont are genuinely different answers.

expected "HCl"·given "hcl" →not accepted

3. Smart match (typo-tolerant)

Compares the answer word by word and scores how close it is, then accepts it if the similarity clears a threshold you set on that question. Loosen it on a history quiz where the spelling of a name is not the point; tighten it where it is.

expected "Pythagoras"·given "Pythagorus" →accepted with typo

Below the accept threshold but still recognisably in the region of right, the answer is marked a partial match and routed to you. It is not scored zero on your behalf.

4. Keyword coverage

For answers that are explanations rather than facts. You list the ideas that have to be present, and set how many of them are enough:

  • All — every keyword must appear.
  • Most — 75% of them.
  • Half — 50%.

A participant who covers the required share is accepted; one who covers noticeably less is flagged as partial and sent to you with the coverage percentage attached, so you can see at a glance what they missed.

keywords: photosynthesis, chlorophyll, sunlight, glucose ·3 of 4 found →accepted at "Most"

5. Numeric equivalence

Numbers are compared as numbers, not as text. Digits, number words and different decimal spellings of the same value are all the same answer.

7=seven =7.0 ·all correct
1500="one thousand five hundred" ·correct

Accepted answers and aliases

Any question can carry a list of acceptable answers rather than one. H2O and water can both be right without you marking forty scripts by hand. On fill in the blanks, each blank keeps its own list, so a sentence with three gaps is really three small questions with three sets of accepted answers.

Partial credit

Where it makes sense, a near answer earns a proportion of the marks instead of all or nothing. Multiple-correct questions score by how many of the right options were found. Fill in the blanks scores per blank. On written answers, partial credit is a setting you turn on per question — some questions deserve it, some genuinely don't.

What the engine will never do

It will never quietly mark a written answer wrong. If a rule does not settle an answer confidently, that answer goes into your review queue with the similarity or coverage score attached. The algorithm's job is to remove the obvious work, not to make the judgement calls.

This is the whole design. Objective questions — multiple choice, true/false, multiple-correct — are marked instantly and you never look at them again. Written answers that clear their rule are marked too. What is left is the small pile that actually needed a person, and that pile is usually four or five answers, not thirty.

Same results in the app and the browser

The grading engine runs in both the Android app and the web quiz-taking page, and the two implementations are kept in lockstep and tested against each other. A written answer scores identically whether it was typed in the app or at quizoma.com/take. Nobody gets a different mark for using a different device.
